If we focus too much on performance goals--targeting a result to measure progress--our risk appetite changes which can lead to compromised reasoning and misplaced focus. Though it comes from management research, not sports science--Ordóñez, Schweitzer, Galinsky, and Bazerman found that having specific targets measurably distorted risk-taking and narrowed attention ([Goals Gone Wild: The Systematic Side Effects of Over-Prescribing Goal Setting](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=35109)). There's no reason to think our golfer brains work differently than our worker brains here.

The risk of many performance goals is that they can adversely impact your game. [Raymond Prior](https://btsmindset.com/) addresses this in Golf Beneath the Surface (another book every golfer should read)--holding tightly to a result you need to happen gets processed by your threat-detection wiring. Standing over a shot thinking about your handicap goal instead of the shot itself is functionally the same problem as a bad lie triggering your amygdala--your attention just left the target. This is playing scared, and it's the goal doing it to you. Now instead of pulling off a shot that we think we need, we are tense which usually achieves the opposite.

"Reduce handicap index by five" is likely a bad goal *by itself*. If you use that goal to guide supporting learning goals--addressing those technical weaknesses--it can be a constructive goal. That breakdown, from a major goal to the supporting goals and tasks underneath it, is the process in [Setting Goals]({% post_url 2026-07-23-goals %}). You are giving yourself a long term goal to inform shorter term goals that will help get us there. But you need to wait for it to happen, not keep it as a focal point. While we generally want our goals to be time-bound, to hold ourselves accountable, that's a riskier proposition with many performance goals.

Another thing to consider is that hitting a number once shows you something (you have the skills to perform at the desired level). But repeatedly hitting a number better demonstrates improvement. Sometimes we need that first time just to boost our confidence. Even if that is the case, it doesn't mean we need to formalize it as a goal.

Use your goals to primarily guide your training plan. Monitor your playing over time to ensure that your goals are helping your game--the goals that keep you practicing are the ones worth keeping, which is the argument in [Choosing the Right Goals]({% post_url 2026-07-27-goal-setting %}). As with things learning-related, experiment to see what works best for you.
